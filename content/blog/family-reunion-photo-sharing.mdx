Your family reunion has a photographer problem and it is not the photography. It is that the three generations in the yard use three completely different sets of tools, and any album you pick will be obvious to one of them and a wall to the other two. Your cousin will not join another group. Your aunt will not install anything. Your grandmother's phone is a phone, and it takes lovely pictures, and that is where they will stay.

The fix is not teaching anybody anything. It is picking the one thing all three groups can already do.

## The reunion's photo problem

A reunion is the event with the widest spread of photo habits in one place. Somebody is shooting portraits on a real camera. Somebody is posting to a private story that expires. Somebody has four hundred photos and no idea how to get one off the phone without sending it in a message. And the person who most wants copies of all of it is usually the one with the least appetite for figuring out how.

So the photos end up split three ways: a folder nobody outside one household can reach, a chat that only half the family is in, and a phone that will be replaced next year with nothing backed up. A year later somebody asks for the picture of the four sisters on the porch, and there are two versions of it, both screenshots.

## The one rule: design for the least technical relative

Pick the person in your family who is least comfortable with a phone and make them the standard. If the plan works for them, it works for everyone.

That rules out most options immediately, and it leaves one thing that everybody can do: point a phone camera at a code and tap what comes up. No download, no store password, no app icon to find later. It opens in the browser they already have. That is the entire skill required, and it is one most people have already learned from a restaurant menu.

The corollary matters too. Whatever you set up, do not ask anyone to remember it. Nobody at a reunion is going to retain a URL, a password, or which of the three albums is the real one.

## Set it up before people arrive

Create the event, name it something a relative would recognize ("Rivera Family Reunion" beats "Reunion 2"), and get the code printed. It takes a minute, and [create your first event](/help/create-your-first-event) walks the screens.

Then make the one call that matters here. New events ask each guest to confirm an email with a one-tap code before uploading, which is a sensible default and still one step too many for some of the people in your yard. For a reunion, turning that off is usually right: with <UiLabel>Require verified emails</UiLabel> off, anyone holding the code types a name and uploads immediately, nothing to confirm. You trade a verified name on each photo for a typed one, which at a family gathering is an easy trade: the photos still say who added them, just without the email step. [How guests join and upload](/help/how-guests-join-and-upload) has both paths if you want to think it through.

<Callout type="tip" title="Print more codes than you think you need">

One on each picnic table, one taped to the drinks cooler, one by the food. A code somebody has to go and find is a code that does not get scanned, and paper is the cheapest thing at this event by a distance.

</Callout>

## During the weekend

Announce it once, at the meal where everyone is sitting down at the same time, and then let it run. One sentence does it: point your camera at the card on the table, add your photos, everybody gets all of them.

Then help the people who need help, in person, once. Sit next to your aunt for a minute, hold the card up, watch her upload one photo, and she will upload forty more over the weekend. That single demonstration beats any instructions you print.

A few reunion-specific things worth catching, because they are the photos people ask for later:

- **The arrival hugs.** They happen over three hours, they involve everyone, and no single person sees more than a third of them.
- **The unposed table.** Halfway through the meal, when nobody is looking at a camera.
- **The generational shots.** Every combination people usually forget to line up: siblings, cousins, all the grandchildren, everyone who shares a name.
- **The house or the place itself.** Somebody will want it in ten years, and nobody photographs it.

## The shoebox

Here is the part most reunions miss. Somebody at that gathering brought a box of prints, or has one at home, and those photos are more at risk than any of the ones being taken this weekend.

Put them in the same album. A phone photograph of an old print, taken flat on a table in good light, is completely usable, and a scan is better if anyone brought one. Files upload up to <UploadSize /> each, so a high-resolution scan is not a problem. Twenty minutes at the kitchen table with the older relatives narrating who is in each one is, quietly, the most valuable thing that will happen all weekend.

Do it while they are all in the same room. That is the whole reason for a reunion.

## After: the archive that stays

When the weekend ends, take a zip of the originals for the family archive and put it wherever your household keeps things that matter. Everything comes out at full quality, the way it was uploaded.

The album itself stays where it is. There is no expiry clock on it and no countdown to a deletion, so the link you sent your cousins works next month and next year, and [why your event album has no expiry date](/blog/event-album-no-expiry-date) explains exactly what that promise is and what its one exception looks like. Practically, it means the reunion album becomes the place the family looks, which means the next reunion starts with people already knowing how this works.
